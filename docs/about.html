About

The project

Propagation Card Reader began as a practical problem at the UBC Botanical Garden Nursery: there were decades of handwritten propagation cards holding hard-won horticultural knowledge—such as sowing dates, treatments, successes, and failures—but no realistic way to digitize them. Commercial OCR meant ongoing cloud costs and sending collection data off-site, while manual entry required staff time the Garden simply didn’t have.

The answer was to run a vision-language model locally. It is free, private, and adept enough at parsing messy handwriting to be genuinely useful. This approach was highly effective because the knowledge on the cards utilizes a relatively restricted sublanguage, and portions of the expected OCR output—specifically accession numbers and taxon name associations—are already known and predictable.

The author

Built by Kevin Kubeck, a horticulturist at UBC Botanical Garden with decades of propagation and plant-collection experience, alongside a self-taught background in Python, data science, and data tooling for conservation.

The first two versions of this project were built independently. This final iteration utilized an agentic coding workflow, leveraging Claude Opus 4.6 as an orchestrator and the local openclaw system as a harness.

Why local-first matters

For a botanical garden, collection data can be highly sensitive, especially when it involves rare-species locations, provenance, and historical institutional records. Keeping the entire OCR pipeline on local hardware ensures the garden never has to trade privacy or budget for the benefits of digitization. That principle is the core motivation behind this tool.
