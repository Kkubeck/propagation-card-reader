How It All Works

The ideas behind the technology, explained through the cards.

This page explains the technical concepts used by the Propagation Card Reader. Each concept is introduced through a concrete example from the project itself. No prior technical knowledge is assumed.

The Basics

What is a model?

In this context, a “model” is a piece of software that has learned patterns from examples. Think of it like a new staff member who has read millions of documents on their first day. They have never seen your specific documents, in this case decades of propagation cards, but they have a general sense of what handwriting looks like, what dates look like, and even what Latin species names might look like.

When you show the model a card image, it draws on that general knowledge to produce a transcription. It is not following a set of rules someone wrote (“if you see ink marks in the top-left corner, that is the species name”). It is recognizing patterns it has seen before in other contexts and applying them to something new.

What are prompts and context?

Before the model attempts to read a card, it receives a prompt. This is a set of specific instructions that guides its focus. Instead of just asking “what does this say?”, the prompt injects expected information, essentially telling the model: “You are looking at a botanical record; expect to find a taxon name and an accession number. What does this say?”

This allows the model to leverage context. Traditional OCR (optical character recognition) looks at characters in isolation: a shape is either a “C” or a “G.” A Vision Language Model (VLM) looks at the entire image and surrounding words. If an ink smudge makes a letter ambiguous, but the surrounding letters are “O T O N E A S T E R”, the VLM uses its understanding of botanical sublanguage to definitively read Cotoneaster. It uses context to read the card more like a person would.

The Hardware Reality

What do “local”, “tokens”, and “RAM” mean?

When we say the model runs “locally,” we mean it runs entirely on the physical computer sitting in front of you—perhaps a repurposed desktop node acting as the system’s brain. The card images are never uploaded to the internet.

This is the opposite of a cloud service, where data is sent to a massive enterprise data center. Cloud models are incredibly fast, but they charge you per token (the fundamental chunks of words a model reads and generates). When running locally, your token cost is zero; you only pay the amortized cost of the hardware and the electricity required to run it.

For a botanical garden, local processing ensures sensitive collection records such as rare species coordinates or donor names remain private. But it comes with a physical limit: RAM (Random Access Memory).

Enterprise cloud models expand across vast arrays of memory. A local machine has a fixed amount of unified memory. To work locally, the entire model must fit inside your machine’s RAM all at once. This requires us to use highly efficient models rather than the massive, resource-heavy versions used by big tech companies.

The DPI Trade-off

DPI stands for dots per inch, measuring how detailed a scanned image is. A higher DPI means more detail, which usually helps the model read finer handwriting.

However, because of the RAM limits on ‘local’ machines there is a strict trade-off. An image scanned at a high DPI creates a much larger file that takes up significant space in the machine’s unified memory. The larger the image footprint, the smaller the model we are forced to use to avoid running out of RAM.

The project settled on 150-175 DPI as the sweet spot: it provides enough detail to accurately read the handwriting while leaving enough memory free for a sufficiently smart model to do the heavy lifting.

The Software Reality

What are “open weights”?

The model used in this project (Qwen 2.5-VL) is described as “open-weight.” This means that the learned parameters, those are the patterns the model extracted from its training data, are freely available for anyone to download and use. You do not need to pay a subscription, sign a license agreement, or use a specific company’s platform.

This open nature is what makes the foundation of the tool portable. If another botanical garden wants to read their own cards, they can download the same model and run it on their own hardware.

However, it is not entirely “plug and play.” Because the system relies heavily on context, another institution would need to do two things to make it work for their records: 1. Personalize the prompts: The instructions must be adjusted to understand the specific layout and sublanguage of their garden’s cards as the current tool is highly tuned to UBCBG’s format. 2. Provide local data: They must supply their own internal collection reports. Specifically the database mapping their accession numbers to taxon names. This powers the Retrieval-Augmented Validation step.

The Processing Logic

How does the model make a decision? (Decision Space & Temperature)

Under the hood, a model doesn’t “read” in the human sense; it calculates mathematical probabilities. For every token it generates, it assigns a probability score to what the next token should be. The range of all possible valid interpretations it considers is its decision space.

We can influence this math using a setting called temperature. A low temperature forces the model to be strict, always picking the single most mathematically probable token. A slightly higher temperature allows it to explore other highly probable paths within the decision space.

This is why a faded, ambiguous card might fail on the first attempt, but succeed on the second. By running the same card multiple times, we sample different paths through the decision space. While there are diminishing returns, eventually the model has exhausted its best guesses during repeated attempts, tending to mathematically converge on the correct answer.

What is RAG and Retrieval-Augmented Validation?

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. In plain terms, it means giving the model a reference sheet to check its work against.

In this project, because the Garden already has a database of every known accession number and recorded species name in its history, we can inject this data into the process. We use a specific technique called Retrieval-Augmented Validation (RAV). When the model reads “Acer macrophyllum,” the system instantly checks that reading against the internal database. If it exists, confidence goes up. If the model reads “Acer macrophylllm” (three L’s), the system catches the likely typo and flags it.

What is post-processing?

The model reads, but the code checks. Post-processing is the secondary layer of code that takes the model’s raw transcription and standardizes it strictly to the Nursery’s sublanguage. It formats the dates, extracts the verified accession numbers, and filters out noise (like geographic coordinates or source codes that might look like accession numbers but aren’t).

Splitting the model’s probabilistic “guessing” from the code’s rigid “checking” is a crucial design decision. It lets each part focus on what it does best.

These concepts are not unique to propagation cards. The same ideas apply to any institution sitting on paper records: herbarium sheets, field notebooks, accession ledgers, library archives. The technology is general. The application here is specific, but the principles transfer.
