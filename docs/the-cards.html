The Cards

Meet the records before meeting the technology.

Before explaining how a computer reads a propagation card, it helps to understand what a propagation card actually is, what information it holds, and why the format is the way it is.

What you are looking at

A propagation card is a small paper record, measuring 8 inches wide by 5 inches tall, that tracks a single propagation event: one batch of seed sown, or one set of cuttings stuck. The card is created when material arrives and is updated by hand as the process unfolds.

Each card records:

  • The identity. The scientific name (genus, species, author), botanical family, and, in most cases, an accession number.
  • Where the material came from. A collector’s name, a collection number, a source institution, or a wild-collection site. Cards might also include extra information for the curator, such as flower color or a form particularity.
  • What was done to it. Sowing date, treatment(s) (cold stratification, soaking, scarification, etc.), growing medium, and location in the nursery.
  • What happened. Germination date, number of seedlings, transplant date, or failure and disposal.

The card is a complete biography of a propagation attempt, from arrival to its ultimate outcome: being planted out in the Botanical Garden.

Two eras of card

The archive this project looked at spans roughly fifty years, and the card format changed partway through. Understanding the difference matters because the computer has to handle both.

Old cards (pre-2017)

The older cards are carbon-copy triplicates, written in pen or pencil. They have about seven rows of printed metadata fields across the top, with handwritten entries filled in. Dates are written as day/month/year. While the accession number format changed twice over five decades, it always maintained a clearly defined field on the right-hand side of the card.

These cards are often faded. The carbon copies, especially the second and third sheets, can be difficult to read even by eye.

Placeholder: image of a dummy old-format card.

New cards (post-2017)

When the garden adopted a new accession management system (IrisBG), the card format was simplified. New cards are printed on card stock with four rows of metadata. Dates are written as year/month/day.

This new format is not inherently “better” or cleaner than the old cards. The simplified layout simply reflects a diminished need to reproduce all the metadata on the physical card, since that information now exists in the digital Collection database. The legibility of these cards still varies entirely based on the handwriting of whoever filled them out.

The hard cases

Most cards follow a straightforward layout: one species, one sowing, a few dates, an outcome. But a significant portion of the archive contains cards that are harder to read, both for humans and machines.

Faded carbon copies

The oldest cards in the archive are carbon triplicates. The top copy is usually readable; the second and third copies can be ghostly. A person squinting at the card might recognize a familiar species name or fill in a date from context. Teaching a computer to do the same is a real challenge.

Condensed propagation tables

As cards piled up over the decades, workers took to condensing information. A propagator would use a single card to create a handwritten propagation table, recording a series of sowings of the same species across different years to allow for rapid comparison of techniques on identical taxa. These multi-sowing cards can contain five, ten, even fifteen separate accessions with their own dates and outcomes, packed into a space designed for a single observation.

These are among the most valuable records in the archive because they show repeated attempts with the same species over time. They are also the hardest for the computer to parse correctly.

Duplexed cards (Two-sided records)

When space ran out on the front of a card, propagators simply flipped it over. Important notes, secondary treatments, continued observations, and expanded propagation tables were frequently written on the back. A system reading these records must be able to link the front and back scans of the same physical card to process it as a single logical document.

No accession number

Before roughly 2012, a propagation attempt did not receive an accession number until it succeeded. If a sowing failed, the card was filed with no accession at all, only a species name.

This means that for the earliest decades of the archive, failed attempts are essentially invisible to the accession database. The card may be the only surviving record that the attempt was ever made. Reading these cards recovers information that otherwise would not exist in any digital system.

Why the cards matter

It is easy to look at a filing cabinet full of handwritten cards and see a data entry problem. But that framing misses the point.

These cards represent institutional memory: the accumulated judgment of every propagator who worked at the garden. Which species need cold stratification. How long to soak a hard-coated seed. Which treatments were tried and failed. When to give up and when to wait another season.

That knowledge is not in any textbook. It was learned by doing, recorded by hand, and filed away. When the person who wrote the card retires, the knowledge does not transfer automatically. It sits in the drawer, waiting for someone to ask the right question.

Beyond capturing techniques, there is the profound possibility of recapturing data that has been “lost” to the current system due to decades of historical data management shifts. There are mature plants growing in the Garden right now whose early histories were severed from their digital records during past database migrations. By digitizing the original paper, we have the promising potential to give these “silent” accessions a voice again, re-rooting them in their own documented history. While we haven’t yet analyzed the resulting database closely enough to find specific examples, the prospect of reconnecting a living collection with its forgotten origins remains one of the most exciting possibilities of this work.

The purpose of this project is to make it possible to ask those questions and recover those stories.
